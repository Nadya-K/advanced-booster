How aesthetic culture erodes identity and creativity

📖 Addicted to Aesthetics (click to expand)

2017 was my most Instagrammable year. I moved from London to San Francisco to New York, changing my life in ways that had to be shared with loved ones but felt impossible to put into words. So I shared photos of it all online: New friends in California, us wine-tasting in Sonoma and shopping in Los Angeles and hiking in Joshua Tree, new friends again in New York, us toasting in Manhattan and dancing in Brooklyn and vacationing on Cape Cod, three different apartments (each typical of an iconic neighborhood), and multiple trips around the Americas for my jet-setting job. When I visited the UK for Christmas, everyone I saw had seen everything I'd done. They also had no idea how I was doing — that the travel made me lonely, the moving made me broke, and so much change made me depressed. Instagram had kept us up-to-date, and out-of-touch. I got home, double-checked everyone's phone numbers, and deleted the app. I haven't shared my life online since.

A lot has been written about how much social media deceives us. We know these apps to be addictive, misleading, and anxiety-inducing. But there is another way in which they distort our understanding of ourselves, each other, and the world: As they become more visual, we become more obsessed with aesthetics, weakening our individuality and creativity just as the world demands our most confident and constructive attention.

As is often the case, we have capitalism to thank for this obsession. Defined as "the branch of philosophy that deals with the principles of beauty and artistic taste", humans used to treat aesthetics — how things look — as a spiritual practice: The Ancient Greek belief that visual symmetry was divine, the Japanese celebration of imperfection in wabi-sabi. But over the past two centuries or so, mass production, advertising, and now digital media have conspired to turn aesthetics into an almost entirely commercial affair.

Now we all think about aesthetics more than we used to. Starting in the early 2010s, members of online "Vaporwave" culture used "A E S T H E T I C" to describe the look and feel of the retro design nostalgia trends they started. As social media became more visual, more sub-cultures started to see themselves as "aesthetics" rather than "scenes". When pandemic lockdowns forced hundreds of millions of us onto TikTok — a completely visual platform — our obsession with creating and finding and replicating aesthetics really took off. In the years since, a new aesthetic has seemed to trend online every month, from "cottagecore" to "clean girl" to "dark academia". Today, Gen Z has redefined "aesthetic" to be not a noun but an adjective for anything aesthetically coherent — anything with a vibe.

Our preoccupation with how things look online — what I'll call aesthetic culture — has been a financial boon for social media companies, individual content creators, and digital design agencies. I myself have made good money helping companies find their place in it. It is also taking a toll on ourselves, our output, and our politics in ways that we can no longer ignore.

At its core, aesthetic culture erodes individual identity. By encouraging us all to build "personal brands", it actually makes it harder for us to understand, like, and protect who we really are. From the posts we curate on Instagram to resumes we publish on LinkedIn, we risk abandoning authenticity to present as coherent, attractive, and aspirational a version of ourselves as possible. Instead of embracing our own contradictions, we mask them in pursuit of visual harmony. Instead of allowing ourselves to change over time, we limit our evolution to whatever makes narrative sense. Instead of being curious about others, we make assumptions based on their own projections.

Despite promising us all creative expression, aesthetic culture really makes us … less creative. Research shows that the more standardized designers' tools become, the more homogenous their work gets. Our increasingly visual social platforms have the same effect: Try as one may to create a niche on these sites, their algorithms inevitably reward expression that is searchable, replicable, and scalable. The result is a world of brands and people that work hard to be different only to look alike.

Aesthetic culture also exploits our human tendency to think that attractive things are also good. Researchers — and technology companies — know that the more visually appealing a thing is, the less likely people are to see problems with it. That's how influencers manage to hide debt and depression behind "aesthetic" day-in-my-life vlogs, and how brands get away with posting stunning recycling campaigns while also filling our oceans with plastic. Our obsession with aesthetics blinds us to challenges we might otherwise creatively solve.

By making us feel less like ourselves and less creative, aesthetic culture helps center consumption as the solution to our woes. It incentivizes us to turn every experience into an image — each image made up of things, each thing available on Amazon.com. The result is a world of content that's really just a world of ads.

So what is to be done? What power do we have? As contributors, the first thing we can all do is share less. We must do more in private — living without photographing, photographing without sharing, sharing without editing. And for those of us who must share online, because livelihoods or craft or community depend on it: Share more truthfully. For individuals, that means documenting things as they are, not as we would like them to look. For brands, it means reflecting what is true as well as what could be.

As consumers, we all need to spend less time scrolling through aesthetic culture, and more time out in the real world. While I left Instagram almost a decade ago, I remain a Pinterest girl to my core. But as that platform's become more "aesthetic" (and full of ads), I've started getting the creative inspiration it used to give me from other, real world sources — quotes from documentaries and books, interior design ideas from visits to museums, fashion inspiration from walks around literally anywhere in New York.

Resisting aesthetic culture isn't just about improving our mental health or protecting our privacy — it's a political act that refuses to contribute to mass delusion. By observing how it works, we can limit its harm, and honor the ideal of Keats' famous poem, in which "beauty is truth, truth beauty, that is all ye know on earth, and all ye need to know".
